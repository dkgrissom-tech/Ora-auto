# How I Structure a Slow-Burn Chapter So the Reader Stays

The risk in slow burn is that nothing happens.

That is fine in theory — readers sign up for the tension — but in
practice, a chapter where two characters almost talk and then do not
can feel like being handed an empty envelope. You signed for a package.
This is not the package.

Here is what I use to make sure something is always moving, even when
the characters are standing still:

**Every chapter changes at least one thing.** Not necessarily a plot
point. An attitude. A small discovery. A shift in what a character
is willing to admit to themselves. Dana does not say anything in
Chapter 4 — but she stops pretending she does not notice when Don's
truck is in the driveway.

**The tension needs a container.** In *Handy Hearts* the container is
the porch repair. Don is there every day for a reason. The work gives
them something to stand next to while they are not quite saying the
things they need to say.

**End on an open question, not a closed door.** Not a cliffhanger.
Just a small thing that requires a next chapter to answer.

Slow burn is not about withholding. It is about earning.

*Handy Hearts* releases September 8. D.K. Grissom.
