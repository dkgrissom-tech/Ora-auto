# How I Draft a Romance Novel with Voice Notes

Most of my best scenes start in a parking lot.

Not at a desk. Not in a coffee shop with aesthetic lighting. In my truck,
between errands, when a line surfaces and I know I have two minutes before
it disappears.

That is why I started recording voice notes. I capture the idea, drop it
into a draft later, and more often than not — the parking-lot version is
better than anything I labored over at the keyboard.

Writing *Handy Hearts* taught me something I should have known earlier:
the scenes that feel most alive came from voice. Dana's grief does not
explain itself in tidy paragraphs. Don's silence does not translate well
to outlines. But if I talk through what is happening between them — just
narrate it like I am telling a friend — the scene finds its shape fast.

Three things that changed how I draft:

**1. Capture before you compose.** The idea is alive right now. Say it
out loud before you try to make it good.

**2. Grief does not monologue.** Dana's sadness comes in small moments,
not grand speeches. Voice notes catch the small moments better than
bullet points.

**3. The edit happens later.** A voice note does not need to be eloquent.
It needs to exist.

If you are a writer who loses best ideas in traffic, try talking them
out loud. You might be surprised what shows up.

3 things I got wrong the first time I wrote a widow heroine:

1. I made her sad. All the time. Nobody is sad all the time. She would
make bad puns about lumber and then feel guilty for laughing.

2. I made grief linear. It does not go in one direction.

3. I let her fall in love to "get over it." That is not how it works.
That is not how *Handy Hearts* works.
